How randomness
learns to flow.

A few colored tiles. A moving distribution. Discover how a model learns to turn random categories into structured patterns—one probabilistic jump at a time.

The idea

Probability moves between patterns.

01

Choose the destination

Our tiny dataset contains just two patterns. The generator should reproduce their frequencies, not merely make each tile look plausible on its own.

02

Design a probability path

At the start, all tiles are random. As time passes, a chosen path puts more probability on patterns that resemble the data.

03

Learn how to jump

The model reads the whole current pattern and predicts a clean endpoint. Those predictions define the rates at which individual tiles change.

02 / Learn

What does the model actually learn?

Learn the probabilities behind a pattern.

Show the model a corrupted pattern and its noise level. Ask: “Which clean pattern could have produced this?” Training nudges its prediction toward the correct clean label.

This browser trains a small logistic table: one logit for each of 16 current states at 25 signal levels. It predicts which of the two clean patterns was used. All clean labels and noisy states are summed exactly, so each training epoch is deterministic full-batch gradient descent.

The two allowed clean patterns are supplied to this toy model; it learns their posterior weights. With such a small dataset we could sample the mixture directly. The longer construction is here to make the mechanics of a generative model visible.

Change the target patterns or their share to reset training. A schedule change does not reset the model: the table is indexed by signal level κ or ᾱ, rather than time.

Try it: choose “Trainable” above, scrub to the end, then train 40 epochs. Compare the empirical bars with the outlined exact path. The table is an educational learner; a large model would use a neural network and sampled training examples.

Loss natsEpochs 0

Loss is averaged uniformly over the 25 signal levels and weighted by the exact noisy-data distribution. An ambiguous input has irreducible uncertainty: a well-trained loss need not reach zero.

03 / The maths

The mechanics beneath the tiles.

Open only as much detail as you need. Symbols match the running example, and the inspector follows the time slider above.

1. Choose a path through probability distributions Tiles jump; probabilities change smoothly
Let Z be a clean four-tile pattern drawn from pdata. Let x be the current pattern. Set κ(0)=0 and κ(1)=1. Each tile is independently retained from Z with probability κ(t), or redrawn as a fair coin otherwise:
qt(x | z) = ∏i=14 [(1−κ(t))/2 + κ(t) 𝟙{xᵢ=zᵢ}]
pt(x) = ∑z pdata(z) qt(x | z)
These equations specify the distribution at each time. A path of distributions alone does not uniquely specify a process: different jump dynamics can share the same marginals. “Retain or redraw” describes how to construct a training example at a chosen time; generation uses the jump process below.
The schedule controls when progress happens. Linear uses κ=t; slow start uses κ=t²; fast start uses κ=2t−t². They share endpoints but have different intermediate distributions and rates.


2. Turn the path into jumps Rates, the master equation, and sampling
For the chosen path, one valid conditional rate changes a wrong tile to its clean value. Averaging these conditional rates over the endpoint posterior gives the marginal rate:
Rt(x, x with tile i flipped | z)
= κ′(t)/(1−κ(t)) · 𝟙{zᵢ ≠ xᵢ}

Rt(x,y) = ∑z p(z | x,t) Rt(x,y | z)
p(z | x,t) = pdata(z) qt(x|z) / pt(x)
All off-diagonal rates are nonnegative. Set R(x,x)=−∑y≠xR(x,y). With row-oriented R and column-vector p, the master equation accounts for incoming minus outgoing mass:
d pt(y)/dt = ∑x≠y pt(x)Rt(x,y) − pt(y)∑x≠yRt(y,x)
Equivalently, dp/dt = Rᵀp.
In a small time interval Δt, a jump x→y has probability approximately R(x,y)Δt. The remaining probability is “stay.” This is a stochastic continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC). A real-valued time parameter does not make the tile values continuous.
The browser uses one-jump Euler steps, with Δt≤1/300 and Δt times the largest total outgoing rate ≤0.075. This ensures valid step probabilities but still has discretization error. It stops at t=0.995; the ideal t→1 limit gives the target distribution. No target pattern is secretly assigned at the end.


3. Learn the denoiser, then assemble the rates Training never needs a full simulated trajectory
Sample a clean Z, a time t, and X from qt(·|Z). Train dθ(Z|X,t) to predict Z. At generation time, substitute dθ for the true posterior in the rate formula. Each prediction uses the entire current four-tile pattern; that is how dependencies between tiles enter the generator.
L(θ) = Et,Z,X[−log dθ(Z | X,t)]
Rtθ(x,y) = EZ∼dθ(·|x,t)[Rt(x,y | Z)]
In large models a neural network predicts categorical token posteriors rather than enumerating every full endpoint. This tiny demo predicts which of two complete clean patterns was used, with a table of logits. That is enough to teach posterior averaging, but it does not demonstrate neural generalization.
Why conditional training works What “same gradients” actually means
Let Z be a clean training pattern. During training we know Z, so we can construct a noisy X at any time and a simple conditional target U(X,Z). During generation Z is unknown. The desired marginal target is its posterior average: u(X) = E[U(X,Z) | X].
E ‖fθ(X) − U(X,Z)‖²
= E ‖fθ(X) − u(X)‖² + E ‖U(X,Z) − u(X)‖²
The cross term vanishes because E[U − u | X] = 0. The last term does not depend on θ. Therefore the two objectives have the same expected parameter gradient, assuming the data/path sampling distribution is independent of θ and the expectations are finite. Two independently sampled minibatch gradients need not be equal. If rates blow up near an endpoint, use an interior time interval or suitable weighting so the loss is finite.
This identity applies to squared-error regression, including finite-dimensional rate targets under those assumptions. The demo actually trains an endpoint classifier with cross-entropy; its justification is the separate identity below. We do not claim every discrete objective is the continuous FM squared loss.
EZ,X[−log dθ(Z | X)]
= EX[H(p(Z|X)) + KL(p(Z|X) ∥ dθ(Z|X))]
Minimizing conditional-label cross-entropy learns the posterior denoiser. The entropy term is independent of θ. Labels give tractable training examples even when the posterior cannot be computed explicitly. Here the posterior is also available exactly because the toy dataset has just two patterns.
“Conditional” here means conditioning the training construction on an endpoint. It is distinct from giving a model a text prompt, reward, or class label at generation time.
How flow matching and diffusion connect Shared ideas, different constructions
QuestionDiscrete flow matchingDiscrete diffusion
Starting designChoose an interpolating probability path and valid jump rates.Choose a corruption process and learn its reversal.
What changes?Categories change by stochastic jumps.Categories change by stochastic transitions.
What is learned here?A clean-endpoint posterior, used to build rates.A clean-endpoint posterior, used to build reverse kernels.
How do we sample here?Approximate a CTMC with small time steps.Sample finite reverse transition kernels.
Are they disjoint?No. Our coordinate-wise uniform flow path is the time reversal of the same corruption marginals used by the diffusion demo when κ(t)=t. The particular flow generator shown is one valid choice; it need not equal the diffusion reverse generator. Matching marginals does not imply matching sample trajectories.
Neither a stochastic discrete flow nor a discrete diffusion smoothly blends two category values. Smooth curves describe probabilities. Both can use denoising prediction; neither is defined solely by the kind of neural network it uses.
